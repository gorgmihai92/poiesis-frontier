# Concepts (philosophy)

Examine the concept itself before designing its form. "What is a button for?" leads to the affordance, not the
component.

1. **Name the thing** and its usual form ("button: a labelled rectangle you click").
2. **What is it for?** The purpose without the form ("to let someone commit to an action, knowing what will
   happen"). Ask "for whom?" and "instead of what?".
3. **Real, perceived, conventional.** What the thing actually allows, what the user perceives it allows, and what
   convention makes them read it that way. On a screen the designer controls only the perceived affordance and
   relies on conventions (Norman 1999).
4. **The distinctions it rests on.** Which categories does the concept take for granted (user and system, input
   and output, page and app, author and reader)? For each, ask what would happen if the line moved or disappeared.
5. **Other forms of the same purpose.** List forms that serve the purpose of step 2 without the usual form
   (a gesture, a default, a conversation, a physical object, nothing at all).
6. **What is lost.** For each form, what the usual form gave that this one does not (feedback, reversibility,
   learnability). That loss is often the assumption to test.

Each new form is a concept and goes through `playbooks/experiment.md`. Concepts that touch how an app looks or
behaves are later built and checked with poiesis-ui and poiesis-ux.
