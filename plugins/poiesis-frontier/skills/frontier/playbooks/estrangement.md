# Estrangement

Make a familiar thing strange, so that what it takes for granted becomes visible and can be changed. Five
operators. Each gives questions and concepts. None gives knowledge about people.

## Read first
Three notes in `research/notes` of the plugin's repository:
- `bell-2005-making-strange`, read in part (the argument and the method; the three accounts of homes were not
  read). Defamiliarisation comes from literary theory: describe a familiar thing so that it has to be looked at
  again. The authors say it is "explicitly not a scientific method" and not a way of understanding real users.
- `djajadiningrat-2000-relabelling-extreme-characters`, read in full. Two techniques from one student project,
  described by the people who used them. Two of their three extreme characters turned out to be stereotypes, and
  the authors apologise for them.
- `pierce-2014-counterfunctional-things`, read in full. Designing by removing a function, tried on cameras and
  shown to 8 people. A design the authors found appealing as an idea was the one every participant called
  useless.

None of the three compares its technique with another way of generating ideas.

## The operators
Use one at a time, and at least two in a session. Write the operator next to everything it produced.

1. **A stranger's description** (Bell, Blythe & Sengers 2005). Describe the thing step by step as someone would
   who has never seen it: from another century, from another place, or "from Mars". Be exact about every detail
   that is usually skipped. Then list what the description makes odd. Each odd detail is something the thing
   takes for granted.
2. **Before and elsewhere** (the same source). How was this done before it was done this way, and how is it done
   somewhere else? Only from the project's research notes or a source, with the note and page; from memory it is
   marked "from general knowledge, unverified". Each difference is a choice that could be made differently
   here.
3. **Relabelling** (Djajadiningrat, Gaver & Frens 2000). Take an unrelated object that can be handled in many
   ways; mechanical things with moving parts worked for the authors. Pretend it is the thing being designed, and
   say what each action on it would do. Keep the actions and drop the object: the mapping need not be
   consistent, and the result need not look like the object.
4. **An extreme character** (the same source). Design for one person with an exaggerated attitude to the task;
   for example someone who trusts nothing they are told, someone who wants never to decide, someone who does
   this a hundred times a day. The character is defined by an attitude to the task and never by a group of
   people; that is where the paper's own characters went wrong. Take what the design reveals to ordinary users
   and leave the character out of the concept.
5. **A counter-function** (Pierce & Paulos 2014). Take a function the thing is known for and remove it or turn
   it round. The paper's three lines, used as a prompt: normally one can …; now one cannot …; but now one might
   …. Note whether the result recalls an older way of doing the thing, which made a limit easier to accept in
   their interviews, and whether it is interesting to use or only interesting to think about.

## Out
- For each operator: what it was applied to, what it made visible, and the concepts that follow.
- Each concept names the assumption the operator exposed, with **Method:** `estrangement (operator)`, and goes
  through `playbooks/experiment.md`.
- These concepts go to people early, at the probe stage: what the designer finds interesting and what people
  would use came apart in the one study that asked.
- No finding about people is reported from any operator. A description by an imagined stranger is the session's
  own text; say so.
