# Lateral thinking

Provocations, reversals and analogies from far-off fields, to get out of the frame the problem came in (after de
Bono). The output is concepts, not judgements: judging comes in `playbooks/experiment.md`.

## Provocation
Write a statement that is deliberately false or impossible about the problem ("the form has no fields", "users
pay us to wait"). Then ask what would follow from it, what principle it suggests, and what useful concept is
nearby. Keep the provocation and the concept apart in the note.

## Reversal
Take each assumption from the frame (or from `playbooks/first-principles.md`) and reverse it: the direction of a
flow, who acts first, who pays, what is fixed and what changes. Keep the reversals that lead somewhere and say
where.

## Analogies
1. **Name the structure** of the problem as relations, without its domain words: "a scarce resource is shared by
   many users who arrive unpredictably", not "booking meeting rooms".
2. **Prompt for analogies on purpose.** People seldom use a distant analogy on their own (Gick & Holyoak 1980).
   Find fields with the same structure: biology, ecology, logistics, games, rituals, law, crafts, other
   industries.
3. **Map relations, not looks.** For each analogy, write what causes what, what depends on what, and what solves
   the problem there; carry over only those relations (Gentner 1983). An analogy that shares only surface features
   is dropped.
4. **Near and far.** Include at least one near analogy (an adjacent field) next to the far ones: in one large study
   ideas from conceptually closer sources were judged more creative (Chan et al. 2015).
5. **Where the analogy breaks.** Name the relation that does not carry over. It is often where the new concept
   needs its own idea.

Each concept goes through `playbooks/experiment.md`.
