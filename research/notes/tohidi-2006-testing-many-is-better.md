# Getting the right design and the design right: testing many is better than one (Tohidi, Buxton, Baecker & Sellen, 2006)

- **Source:** Proceedings of CHI 2006: 1243–1252 · https://doi.org/10.1145/1124772.1124960 (pages = PDF pages; add
  1242 for the proceedings' printed page)
- **File:** `../papers/tohidi-2006-getting-right-design.pdf` (an author's copy, from billbuxton.com)
- **Licence:** © ACM 2006; the author's copy is free to read, not open-licence: local only.
- **Evidence:** empirical · one experiment between groups, 48 students and recent graduates of one university, 12
  per condition; paper prototypes of one system, a home thermostat (p. 2–3).
- **Read:** full text, p. 1–10, on 2026-11-22.

## What it says
- Three designs did the same things in three different styles. A person either tried one of them, or tried all
  three, and then rated and discussed what they had tried (p. 2–4).
- **Ratings were higher for a design seen alone** than for the same design seen among three: 9.08 against 8.13,
  8.83 against 8.39, and 7.92 against 6.89 on a ten-point scale. Two of the three differences were significant
  (p = 0.004 and 0.014, one-tailed); the middle one was not (p = 0.064) (p. 4–5).
- **Positive comments fell** for all three designs when they were seen among three. **Negative comments rose**
  significantly only for the design rated lowest (p. 5–6). Comments were counted on 24 of the 48 recordings, six
  per condition (p. 5).
- **Suggestions did not increase.** People who saw three designs offered no more substantial ideas for
  improvement than people who saw one, against the authors' prediction (p. 6). Their reading: a test finds
  problems; it does not produce solutions (p. 1, 9).
- None of the 36 people who saw one design rejected it. Three of the 12 who saw three rejected one of them
  outright (p. 9).
- The authors do not claim to know which comments were the valid ones; they assume the reactions of people who
  saw three are better informed (p. 7).

## What it means for the plugin
- The study playbook shows people two or three variants, not one. A concept shown alone collects politeness: its
  ratings are higher and nobody rejects it.
- A session with people is asked for problems and preferences, not for design ideas.
- One experiment, students, one kind of product, twelve people per group and six per group for the comments.
  Several tests were run with no correction for their number, and the rating tests are one-tailed. The direction
  is consistent across the three designs; the size of the effect is not something to plan on.

## Quotes
- "when presented with a single design, users give significantly higher ratings and were more reluctant to criticize than when presented with the same design in a group of three" (p. 1)
- "It is a means to identify problems, not provide solutions." (p. 1)
- "three out of the twelve participants in the Multiple design condition explicitly rejected one of the prototypes viewed" (p. 9)
