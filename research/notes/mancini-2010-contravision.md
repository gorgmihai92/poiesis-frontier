# ContraVision: exploring users' reactions to futuristic technology (Mancini, Rogers, Bandara, Coe, Jedrzejczyk, Joinson, Price, Thomas & Nuseibeh, 2010)

- **Source:** Proceedings of CHI 2010: 153–162 · https://doi.org/10.1145/1753326.1753350 (pages = the
  proceedings' printed pages)
- **File:** none; read in the ACM Digital Library's reader.
- **Licence:** © ACM 2010: local only.
- **Evidence:** empirical, qualitative · 16 people in four groups of four, aged from their late twenties to their
  late seventies; two groups saw one version of a ten-minute video and two saw the other; individual interviews,
  then a group discussion (p. 157). One imagined technology, a diet monitor with camera glasses and an implant.
- **Read:** full text, p. 153–161, on 2026-11-22. Not read: the reference list.

## What it says
- **The method:** show a technology that does not exist yet in two versions of the same story, one where it goes
  well and one where it goes badly. The two versions keep the same subject, style, scenes, characters and
  places, and differ in how the characters take the technology and in how things end (p. 153–154).
- Both versions drew a wide range of concerns, and many were the same. Some came up after only one version:
  what else the recorded information could be used for, the stress of hiding the device, and self-knowledge
  came only from people who saw the negative one; caution about trusting a new technology too easily came
  mostly from people who saw the positive one (p. 158–160).
- People pushed back against the version they saw. Viewers of the positive version found the main character too
  trusting; most viewers of the negative version said the system could be trusted and he should have stuck with
  it (p. 158).
- The authors' conclusion: two comparable versions draw a wider range of reactions than one version can
  (p. 153, 161).
- Limits they state: even with two versions it is hard to tell which reactions are to the technology and which
  to the way it was shown; how far apart the two should be is left open (p. 161).
- The method is meant for something that can only be shown through a representation and is likely to touch
  sensitive matters. It also works with written scenarios or storyboards, which cost little. Where a working
  prototype exists, that may be the better thing to show (p. 161).

## What it means for the plugin
- The speculative playbook stages an artefact in two contrasting versions built on the same scene, and records
  which reactions came from which version.
- A reaction is a reaction to the telling as much as to the thing. A concern that appears under one version only
  is noted as such, and is a lead, not a finding about the technology.
- Sixteen people, one technology, each group seeing one version. The study shows that the two versions drew
  different concerns. It did not compare two versions with one version shown to as many people, so "wider than
  a single version" is the authors' reading of their groups, not a measured difference.

## Quotes
- "the use of two systematically comparable representations of the same technology can elicit a wider spectrum of reactions than a single representation can" (p. 153)
- "Where working prototypes are available, these may be preferable." (p. 161)
