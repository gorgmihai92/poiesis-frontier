# Parallel prototyping leads to better design results, more divergence, and increased self-efficacy (Dow, Glassco, Kass, Schwarz, Schwartz & Klemmer, 2010)

- **Source:** ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction 17(4), article 18, 24 pages ·
  https://doi.org/10.1145/1879831.1879836 (pages = PDF pages)
- **File:** `../papers/dow-2010-parallel-prototyping.pdf` (the authors' copy, from hci.stanford.edu)
- **Licence:** © ACM 2010; the authors' copy is free to read, not open-licence: local only.
- **Evidence:** empirical · one experiment between groups, 33 people (16 and 17), average age 22, three quarters
  of them students, none a professional designer; one task, a web banner advertisement, in one session of 150
  minutes (p. 5–6, 9).
- **Read:** full text, p. 1–21, on 2026-11-22. Not read: the last appendix (the design brief) and the reference
  list (p. 21–24).

## What it says
- Everyone made five prototypes and a final advertisement, with the same time and the same amount of critique.
  The only difference was when critique came: after each prototype (serial), or after three and then after two
  (parallel) (p. 2, 5).
- **Results favoured parallel on every measure.** Clicks per million impressions over the campaign: 445.0
  against 397.9. In the first five days, with about equal impressions: 44 clicks against 26 (p. 11–12). Expert
  ratings out of 50: 24.4 against 21.7 (p. 12). All at p < 0.05.
- **Parallel prototypes were more varied:** raters judged a serial participant's prototypes more alike (3.25
  against 2.78 on a seven-point similarity scale) (p. 13).
- **Variety did not predict quality.** A participant's similarity rating predicted neither clicks nor expert
  ratings (p. 13). Participants with experience made better advertisements and less varied prototypes (p. 10,
  15, 17).
- Confidence in the task rose in the parallel group (2.5 points of 20) and stayed level in the serial group
  (p. 14). Eight of 17 serial participants called the critique negative; none of the 16 parallel ones did (p. 17).
- The authors' explanations (comparison teaches, exploration before refinement, distance from one's only idea)
  are offered as explanations, not tested here (p. 15–17).
- They name what is not known: other contexts, longer time scales, teams (p. 19).

## What it means for the plugin
- The study playbook has the maker build two or three variants before showing any of them, and collect the
  responses to all of them together. Critique that arrives after each variant pulls toward repairing that one.
- Variety is a means, not a score: in this study the more varied sets were not the better ones person by person.
  The rule on reporting distinct ideas says how many there were; it does not say more is better.
- One experiment, novices, one kind of task, groups of 16 and 17, and several results just under p = 0.05. It
  supports trying variants in parallel; it does not size the benefit for another task.

## Quotes
- "Parallel participants outperformed serial participants by all performance measures" (p. 2)
- "Similarity ratings were not predictive (or inversely predictive) of online click performance" (p. 13)
- "An important direction for future work is to study the impact of parallel design in other contexts, especially for longer time scales and for design teams." (p. 19)
