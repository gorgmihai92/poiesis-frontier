# Research through design fiction: narrative in real and imaginary abstracts (Blythe, 2014)

- **Source:** Proceedings of CHI 2014: 703–712 · https://doi.org/10.1145/2556288.2557098 (pages = the
  proceedings' printed pages)
- **File:** none; read in the ACM Digital Library's reader.
- **Licence:** © ACM 2014: local only.
- **Evidence:** analytical · one author's reflective essay on a technique he used in one project (about a dozen
  imaginary abstracts), plus a word count of 100 real abstracts. The paper calls itself "primarily a provocative
  argument" (p. 704) and says it does not report empirical findings (p. 707).
- **Read:** full text, p. 703–710, on 2026-11-22. Not read: the reference list (p. 710–712).

## What it says
- An imaginary abstract summarises the findings of a paper that was never written, about a prototype that does
  not exist and a study that never took place (p. 703, 707).
- It copies the three parts found in real abstracts of design research: a frame that sets the problem, a
  prototype and a study, and findings that feed a discussion (p. 704–705).
- Writing the imagined findings moves attention from how to build the thing to what building and testing it
  could teach. The fiction "forces the question – would this be worth doing?" (p. 708).
- The imagined studies had the prototype received badly as well as well, so the abstract shows what a failure
  would be worth (p. 708).
- In the project the abstracts did three jobs: they archived concepts, they helped spot ideas probably not worth
  taking further, and they showed whether a prototype would be likely to answer the research question (p. 709,
  710).
- A deliberately vague description was misread by a collaborator into a different, working prototype, which the
  author counts as a benefit (p. 708).
- **It is not a result.** The paper says it does not advocate faking studies or making up findings, that fiction
  and real results are not equivalent, and that its abstracts mark themselves as fiction in the title and by
  exaggeration (p. 710).

## What it means for the plugin
- A step in the speculative-research playbook: before a study is designed, write the abstract of the paper it
  would lead to, once with the result hoped for and once with the opposite. If neither version would teach
  anything, the study is not worth running.
- Every imagined abstract is labelled as fiction in its first line and is never saved where results are kept.
- One author, one project, his own account. It shows the technique can be used and what he got from it; nobody
  compared projects with and without it.
