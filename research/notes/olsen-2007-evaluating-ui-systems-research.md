- **File:** none; read in the ACM Digital Library's reader.
- **Licence:** © ACM 2007: local only.
- **Evidence:** analytical · a position paper by one author, about toolkits and interface architectures. It says
  its criteria "are not novel"; the opinions are the author's own (p. 257–258). No data.
- **Read:** full text, p. 251–258, on 2026-11-22.

## What it says
- "Simple usability testing is not adequate for evaluating complex systems" (p. 251).
- A usability experiment rests on three assumptions: people can walk up and use the thing with little training;
  the task is standard, with little variation between people; and a test fits in one or two hours (p. 252–253).
  Work on toolkits and architectures rarely meets any of the three (p. 253).
- **The usability trap:** answering "if it can't be measured it is not research" reduces research to what is
  trivially measurable (p. 253). The paper asks not to create only what a usability test can measure (p. 257).
- **The fatal flaw fallacy:** a small team that reaches into new territory always leaves something out. If the
  evaluation asks "what does it not do", nothing new ever passes (p. 253).
- Evaluate against the claim being made. The paper lists claims and how to show each: importance, a problem not
  solved before, generality, less effort to try many solutions, new people able to take part, power in
  combination, scaling up (p. 254–257).
- When the claim is that something can now be done that could not be done before, a usability test is beside the
  point (p. 254).
- If a difference has to be analysed carefully for statistical significance to show at all, it is probably not
  important (p. 254). The author's rule of thumb is that people change tools only for a large improvement, about
  twice as good (p. 254, 256).
- A new work should not have to pass every criterion either; the question is whether important progress was made
  (p. 257).

## What it means for the plugin
- The experiment playbook first asks what the concept claims. The test follows from the claim: "this can now be
  done at all" is shown by a demonstration, "this is faster" by a timed comparison.
- An early concept is not killed for a feature it lacks. The kill criterion is about the assumption the concept
  rests on, not about completeness.
- The paper is about tools for programmers and designers, and it is one author's argument. It gives reasons, not
  measurements, and applying it to early concepts in general is this plugin's step, not the paper's.
