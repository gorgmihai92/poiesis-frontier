## What it says
- The term comes from literary theory (Shklovsky, 1917). Defamiliarization is a device that makes the reader look
  again at something so familiar that it seems natural and beyond question (p. 150–151).
- Ways the paper shows of making the familiar strange: describing it through the eyes of someone who has never
  seen it; an ethnographer's flat description of one's own customs; a history of how it came to be; a comparison
  with how it is done elsewhere (p. 151–152, 166).
- Asking what an everyday thing is for is itself a defamiliarization. The authors' example is Norman's question
  about glass (p. 153).
- It "is explicitly not a scientific method": it does not aim at a better understanding of real users. It gives
  designers a lens on their own practice (p. 154).
- The authors turn their accounts into twelve statements that reverse common design goals, among them
  "Efficiency is overrated", "The user is plural" and "Not everyone has broadband" (p. 166–169).
- No travel is needed: it is first a way of writing, a rich description that makes the familiar strange. The
  school exercise is to describe a thing to someone from Mars, or as someone from Mars (p. 169–170).
- It asks for rigour and attention to the details usually taken for granted (p. 170).

## What it means for the plugin
- One operator in the estrangement playbook: describe the thing as a stranger would, then list what the
  description makes questionable. Each questioned detail is an assumption a concept can remove or reverse.
- The paper says outright that this is not a way of learning about users. A defamiliarized description produces
  questions and concepts, never a finding about people.
- The three accounts of homes were not read, so nothing here rests on them.

## Quotes
- "Defamiliarization is explicitly not a scientific method" (p. 154)
- "It is essentially a rich description which renders strange the familiar." (p. 170)
